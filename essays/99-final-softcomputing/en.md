# The Geometry of Intelligence: The Soft Mathematics of an Imprecise Person

*An essay about what to work with when precision isn't coming. Fourth in the series: the first argued that you cannot finish reading another person; the second, that instead of reading, people finish writing them; the third, that accuracy will rise but hit a wall — and that the wall isn't made of instruments.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 0. The Opening Scene

I was filling out a page verification request on Facebook. An ordinary form, nothing personal. One field: "Category." A dropdown with forty entries.

I froze.

I run a Telegram channel about news in Latvia. Is that journalism? The list has "News & Media." But I'm not a newsroom, I don't have the Saeima in my contacts, I just sit by Ķīšezers and try to explain what's going on without the hype. Is it a blog? There's "Digital creator." But I don't shoot reels, I write pieces of fifteen hundred characters. An entrepreneur? That was on the list too.

The system required one. One word that would describe me most accurately.

And that's where it broke. I knew enough about myself — the problem wasn't ignorance. The problem was the format of the answer. Any checkbox would have been a lie, not because I'm not that, but because I'm several things at once.

I chose "Digital creator." The form lit up green. "Great, now we know who you are." I took a screenshot of that green "Great" and saved it as `category.png`.

At that moment I understood what this whole text would be about. Not that I don't know who I am. That I do know — and that the knowledge doesn't fit into a number, or a word, or a checkbox. And if an honest answer doesn't fit the form, it isn't the answer that should change.

## 1. The Thesis, Up Front

I'll start with the claim and explain afterward — the same as in the third essay. If the thesis doesn't hold, you can stop reading.

The three previous texts proved the same thing three independent ways. First: the description of a person doesn't converge — however much you refine it, the picture doesn't settle onto a limit, it keeps branching. Second: a label doesn't describe a person, it steers whoever reads it. Third: the accuracy ceiling has an irreducible residual, and that residual is not instrument noise but the signature of the reconstruction procedure itself.

After three such passes a question arises that can no longer be avoided. **If precision is unreachable in principle — what do you work with?**

The answer I'll be defending goes like this: you work with instruments that don't require precision on the input. Such instruments exist, there are many of them, they are gathered into a class, the class has a name — **soft computing** — and inside it there is no philosophy but mathematics: fuzzy sets, probabilistic reasoning, dynamical methods. All of these are disciplines with theorems, not with moods.

One common feature unites them, and it's the reason to keep reading. **They do not eliminate uncertainty. They represent it honestly.**

The difference between "eliminate" and "represent" is this entire text. The classical approach treats imprecision as an enemy: interference, a measurement defect, something that will go away with a better instrument. The soft approach treats it as a property of the object: if the object has no sharp boundaries, then an honest answer about it must have no sharp boundaries either, and the task is not to draw the boundary in but to write its absence down in a form you can compute with.

This claim has two opponents, not one. That's what distinguishes this text from the previous ones, and its whole structure rests on it.

**The opponent on the right — the precision camp.** Anyone who believes the right answer to "what am I like" is a number, a type, a category. That a fuzzy answer is an admission of defeat, a stopgap until a good test arrives. To him I answer: the test isn't coming, and not because science is poor.

**The opponent on the left — the fog camp.** The popular literature about "embracing uncertainty," where exactly the same words are used with no content at all. "Work with chaos," "embrace your ambiguity," "trust the process." That is one step from the codebook of the second essay: a beautiful label that measures nothing and therefore cannot turn out to be wrong.

Between them there is a narrow strip, and it is occupied by real mathematics. This whole text is an attempt to stand on that strip and not once fall into either pit. Hence the criterion by which I should be judged: **if after reading you cannot tell a soft method from soft talk — I have failed.**

And a promise up front, so there's no misunderstanding. Formalism is coming. Not consolation, not "accept yourself as you are," not permission to skip the details. Formulas, numbers, other people's measurements, and one hypothesis with explicitly named conditions under which it should be considered dead.

## 2. Who This Is Addressed To: Yourself First, Then Others

Before unfolding the apparatus, I should say what it's applied to. The order here is neither accidental nor modest.

**Everything that follows is an instrument of self-knowledge first. Only secondarily does it transfer to knowing another person.**

There are four reasons, and the first looks counterintuitive.

**First: you have the most data about yourself — and the ceiling is still there.** You have observed yourself all your life, from the inside, continuously, including things invisible from outside. If precise description doesn't converge even here, then the excuse "I haven't observed enough" doesn't work. So self-knowledge is not the easiest case but the strongest test.

**Second: this isn't reasoning, it's been measured.** And the measurement hits harder than you'd expect.

**\[textbook\]**

There is a study with a round design: a person rates himself, four friends rate him, up to four strangers rate him, and all of it is checked against behavioral criteria. There were a hundred and sixty-five participants. The result was neither "the observer is more accurate" nor "you are more accurate," but **depends on the axis**.

On traits that are poorly visible from outside — anxiety and everything that happens internally — a person knows himself better than any observer. On traits loaded with evaluation — intelligence above all — friends are more accurate than he is. And on something like sociability everyone is roughly equal.

Think about what that means. Maximum data does not yield maximum knowledge. On evaluative axes additional observations don't help at all — and you have infinitely many of them, you observe yourself continuously. The ceiling doesn't stand where the data runs out.

**Third: a concrete prescription follows from this, not a consolation.**

**\[framework\]**

Here I move from a measured fact to my own inference from it, and I want to mark that seam directly, because the series requires such seams to be marked. The asymmetry of accuracy is an established fact. What I say next is my inference, measured by no one.

If the blind spot depends on the axis, then a fuzzy answer about yourself should have **different widths on different axes**. The band of uncertainty must be wider precisely where the trait is more heavily loaded with evaluation. That tells you something concrete: which of your self-assessments not to trust, and which to outsource to an observer. Not "be more modest" — but "on these axes your estimate is systematically worse than someone else's, ask your friends."

And a caveat right away, so you don't have to go looking for it in the inset. **Nobody has measured the width of that band.** The asymmetry of accuracy has been measured — someone else's result, with a sample, a procedure, and published numbers. That a band changing width from axis to axis follows from it is my claim. It's a conjecture, and it awaits testing on the same footing as everything else in this text marked as framework.

> **Math inset 1. Band width as a function of the axis.** \[framework\] Usually the uncertainty of self-assessment is thought of as a single number: "I know myself to about such-and-such a degree." From the asymmetry something else follows: the width of the band is a function of the axis, **w = w(axis)**, and it grows with how heavily the trait is loaded with evaluation. On axes of low observability — where you are the only witness — the band is narrow. On axes where a trait is both visible from outside and flattering or unpleasant from inside, the band is wide, and its center is shifted besides. This gives a fuzzy answer about yourself a **shape**, not just blurriness in general: it has a profile across axes, and the profile is predictable in advance. The obligatory caveat: what has been measured is the asymmetry of accuracy; that width depends on the evaluativeness of an axis is my inference from it. Nobody has measured the width of the fuzzy band of self-assessment.

**Fourth: a soft answer in the first person is operational; in the third person it is merely descriptive.**

There is nowhere to apply someone else's membership function. You are not going to treat a person "at 0.6" — it changes nothing in what you do. But your own — you will. A distribution instead of a point changes which tasks you set yourself, where you plan for a breakdown in advance, which of your own states you don't trust when making decisions. It changes behavior, and that makes it an instrument rather than a description.

And one last thing. The third essay honestly admitted that its part on self-application opens not the same lock but the one next door: the question "can I finish reading myself" turned out not to be the same as "can I finish reading another." This text picks up the door next door and makes it the main one — explaining in retrospect why the series walked up to it at all.

## 3. The Fork: Chaos and Fuzziness Are Different Things

**\[textbook\]**

Everything that follows depends on not confusing two kinds of imprecision. So I'll clear the fork immediately, in the first paragraphs — the way the second essay had to clear up the tropical zodiac misunderstanding right away.

**Chaos** is when the rule is sharp and prediction is impossible. The system is fully deterministic: if you knew the initial state with absolute precision, you could compute the entire future. But the slightest imprecision on the input grows exponentially with time, and after a while the forecast turns into noise. That's the strange attractor from the first essay. There is no blurriness here at all: the boundaries are sharp, the rules are sharp, what is unpredictable is the trajectory.

**Fuzziness** is exactly the reverse. No dynamics, no time. The boundaries of a category are simply blurred, and membership is partial. The question "does this object belong to category A" has no answer "yes" or "no," and not because you're missing something, but because the category itself is built that way. That's the convex mixture of archetypes from the second essay.

And here I have to make a caveat that looks pedantic at first glance but in fact holds up the whole part.

In the second essay the mixture was **convex**: the shares divided one. More in one vertex meant less in another, always a hundred percent in total. That construction is called a simplex, and it is the formal signature of a probabilistic object. That is exactly how a probability distribution is built.

A fuzzy set carries no such condition. Memberships in different categories are **independent**, and their sum is constrained by nothing. A person can be strongly in one vertex and simultaneously strongly in another, and that is not an arithmetic error.

The difference looks like an arithmetical trifle, but in fact it is the passage from "a person is distributed among castes" to "a person partially belongs to each." The second is stronger and closer to what I want to say. This essay weakens the construction of the second one by removing its normalization.

> **Math inset 2. A mixture without normalization.** \[textbook\] In the second essay a person was described as a mixture of archetypes with weights summing to one: **∑ᵢ wᵢ = 1**, **wᵢ ≥ 0**. That's a simplex — the same structure as a probability distribution, and it is where the intuition "more of one means less of another" came from. A fuzzy set removes exactly one condition: the normalization. What remains is **μᵢ ∈ [0,1]** for each category, and **no link at all between the different μᵢ**. Formally one line is gone. In substance the budget is gone: ceasing to be 0.3 sociable no longer means becoming 0.7 withdrawn. Both memberships can be high, both low, any values at all. You can check this on yourself in a second. I am a Rigan to about 0.7 and a Russian-language author to about 0.8, and there is no point adding those numbers — the vertices do not share a common budget. In a simplex such a pair is impossible; in a fuzzy set it is unremarkable. Hence the shortest answer to the objection "fuzziness and probability are the same thing": for probabilities the sum is fixed at one, for memberships it is free. The difference is not philosophical but checkable by addition.

The claim of this part: **the object-function carries both kinds of imprecision at once, and each has its own separate rigorous apparatus.** Different disciplines, different theorems, different conditions of applicability. Gluing them together would be an invitation for a mathematician to pin me to the wall — as already happened with the fractal in the first essay, where the image ran ahead of the formalism.

## 4. A Check Before the Apparatus: No Boundaries Were Found

**\[textbook\]**

Before building mathematics for blurred boundaries, I have to answer an honest question. Otherwise it will hang over the whole text and spoil every paragraph that follows.

**What if the boundaries are there after all — and we simply searched badly?**

What if people really do divide into sorts, and the continuity I'm about to describe is not a property of the object but our own indecision? Not an admission that we failed to find the threshold?

The question is not rhetorical. And there's no need to guess — in psychometrics there is a discipline that spent thirty years on exactly this, and the results are published.

It's called **taxometrics**, Paul Meehl invented it, and its question is word for word ours. Is the latent variable behind the observed signs a category with a real boundary, or a continuous dimension? More simply: does a threshold exist by which people divide into two sorts, or is the difference only one of degree? So much work has accumulated that it has been summarized quantitatively — and the combined samples of those summaries exceed half a million people.

Here's what came out.

**The large 2012 review** — a hundred and seventy-seven articles, three hundred and eleven separate findings, five hundred and thirty-three thousand participants in total. Taxonic findings accounted for 38.9%. But such findings are noticeably rarer in later and methodologically stronger studies, and in those reporting comparative fit indices based on simulated comparison data; once these and other confounds were statistically controlled, the true prevalence was estimated at about **14%**. The nature of the two numbers must not be confused, and it differs even more than it looks. 38.9% is not a measurement of structure but a tally of other people's conclusions: the proportion of findings that **the original authors judged** to be taxonic. 14% is the output of a model after control. Neither of the two is a direct observation of categories.

**The 2020 meta-analysis** did the same work more strictly, and there both proportions are observed. It found 279 articles and 502 findings in all. The meta-analysis proper covered the 183 articles and 317 findings that used a comparative fit index; the remaining 185 findings from 96 articles, which used none, were taken for comparison. And here is the main thing. Among studies **with** the index, taxonic findings run at 16.7% (53 of 317). Among studies **without** it — 57.8% (107 of 185). Controlling for construct domain, studies with the index are 4.88 times more likely to yield a dimensional result. No content domain showed aggregate support for the taxonic model, and across the corpus as a whole dimensional findings outnumber taxonic ones **five to one**.

**\[+psych. 29.09\] These two numbers are worth holding in mind, but not as a coincidence.** The 2012 review asked: how many taxonic findings would remain if study quality had been uniformly high and the comparative fit index always used? The model answered: about 14%. The 2020 meta-analysis went and looked at the studies where the index actually was used: 16.7%. These are not two independent roads to one number — the 2020 corpus contains the 2012 one, and the second quantity is a direct measurement of what the first estimated. It is a prediction and its check, eight years apart, two and seven tenths of a point apart. That is more modest, and firmer.

**The review specifically on personality**, rather than clinical work, yields a pair of numbers worth writing this whole part for. A hundred and two studies, a hundred and ninety-four results, and they split into two groups. Some studies compared their curves against simulated data of **two kinds**: data simulated from a category with a real boundary, and data simulated from a continuous dimension — and asked which of the two the real data resembled more. This is not a check for false positives but a choice between two models, and it has a scale: one end is confidently a category, the other confidently continuous, the middle is "we don't know." The others did no such check at all.

Here it is. Those that didn't check declared types in **sixty cases out of ninety-two**. Those that did — in **four out of a hundred and two**.

Sixty out of ninety-two against four out of a hundred and two. The denominators differ because the hundred and ninety-four results split unevenly into those two groups: ninety-two in studies without the check, a hundred and two in studies with it.

**An impressive reversal suggests itself here, and I'm going to decline it.**

The temptation runs like this. Usually improving a method finds **more** structure: a finer instrument, more visible detail; a microscope shows cells where the eye saw uniform tissue. And here it came out the other way round — categories melted as the procedure got stricter. Elegant.

And wrong. That sentence hides an assumption: that structure necessarily means categories. But **a continuous scale is structure too**, and fully so. A more accurate method does not find "more structure" — it shows more accurately what structure is actually there, and that may turn out to be anything at all. The formulation was taken away from me by Nick Haslam, the author of the reviews the figures above come from, and taken away justly.

What remains after the correction? The result remains, and it didn't suffer from the correction — it got sharper.

**What melted was not structure. What melted was the assumption that structure has to come in sorts.**

For a hundred years we asked the data "what types do people divide into," and as long as we asked sloppily, the data obligingly answered. Once we asked strictly, it turned out the question itself was badly posed. Not "how many types" but "is there a boundary." No boundary was found. What was found was a continuous difference of degree — precisely the form of answer I will be arguing for from here on. And this is not an empty result: the instrument did not throw up its hands, it was choosing between two models and chose one.

And let me close the obvious objection at once: aren't the strict studies confounded with the late ones, and the lax with the early? They are. But asked about this directly, Haslam replied that the era does not explain the decline, and gave two reasons: the variables studied before and after are roughly the same, and the samples in later work grew larger — so the later results should be **more** reliable, not less. How large a share is attributable to method specifically he declined to put a number on, and neither will I.

**And a bridge to the second essay, now an empirical one.** The sixteen Jungian types — that very codebook — were tested taxometrically across three different questionnaires. No taxon was found on any of them: preferences behave like continuous dimensions. In the second essay I took the codebook apart logically: a short alphabet, four bits, a label that steers whoever hung it. Here it is taken apart by measurement — they looked for a boundary between castes and did not find one. **A mixture instead of a caste turned out not to be my preference but what the data showed.**

Now three caveats, without which this becomes overselling.

**First, and it had to be rewritten toward strength rather than caution.** I was going to say "taxa exist, there are just few of them": the 2012 review named several areas where the evidence looked encouraging. But by 2020 the conclusion had hardened, and the author put it to me directly: **no variable in psychology has shown consistently replicable categorical structure.**

**\[+psych. 29.09\] And now that sentence has to be propped up, because the abstract of the same paper names six variables as taxon candidates "based on a preponderance of independently replicated findings."** There is no contradiction, and the author resolves it himself. A preponderance means that a majority of findings for that variable came out taxonic. Consistency means it comes out that way always or almost always. The last paragraph of the Results section shows how different those are: for five of the six candidates the plausible taxonic findings have been replicated **exactly once**, and for the sixth, taxonic findings outnumber non-taxonic ones four to three. A preponderance is not a consistency.

**\[+math. 29.09\] And two remarks about that six, both in my favour, which is why I'll make them carefully.** First: not one of the six is a personality trait — they are alcohol use disorder, intermittent explosive disorder, problem gambling, autism, suicide risk and pedophilia. On the territory where I carry the weight there are no candidates at all. Second: in discussing the candidates, biological sex was set aside on a separate line as a non-psychological variable. So it was there — which means the procedure is not blind: where a category is real, it finds it. But the conclusion goes no further than that, and I won't stretch it. Biological sex is a taxon of enormous separation, and an instrument that resolves the enormous may fail to resolve the moderate. How sensitive the method is to a taxon of modest size is a question of **power**, and these data do not settle it. So the six remove only the crudest objection — "the method is built to return 'continuous' no matter what." They do not remove the subtler one: "the method may not have reached." **\[framework\]** — because the step from "the procedure is not blind" to "the negative result is contentful" is mine, not the authors', and I now take it in the narrow version.

The difference between "there are few" and "none replicated consistently" is substantial, and the second formulation is more accurate. But it too does not mean "taxa don't exist" — it means none has held up under re-examination. This is a claim **about the state of the evidence, not about the nature of things**, and it has to be taken exactly that way. Not "everything is continuous," but "categorical structure has nowhere replicated consistently." Otherwise my own thesis becomes a codebook, exactly as warned in part seven.

**Second: the literature is mostly clinical, and that is not my territory.** I carry the weight on the personality slice. About the clinical part I'll say one sentence: the same thing turned up there too, where categories are fixed in diagnostic manuals. Listing diagnoses in a text about fuzziness would drag the reader into someone else's painful subject, one with no bearing on the thesis. **\[+math. 29.09\]** And here the obvious objection is disposed of — "the literature is clinical, so it doesn't transfer to personality." The 2020 meta-analysis tested exactly that and answered directly: there are systematic differences among seventeen construct domains, but psychopathology was **no more likely** to generate taxonic findings than normal variation — individual differences in personality, response styles, gender and sexuality. The transfer is not mine, it is theirs. And separately, so that this does not quarrel with the caveat above: the few findings that looked encouraging in the 2012 review fell **not on normal personality**. What the author called promising, though not definitive, was confined there to schizotypy, substance use disorders and autism, while normal personality he placed explicitly among the domains yielding little persuasive evidence of taxa. So even the weak version of the concession was about someone else's territory.

**\[+psych. 29.09\] And what became of those three by 2020 — I'll say it more precisely than I said it before, because before I said it wrongly.** Two of the three, autism and substance use in the form of alcohol dependence, remained among the six candidates; schizotypy is not among them. But remaining a candidate is not the same as being confirmed: as said above, each of the six rests either on a single replication or on a four-to-three count. They were not refuted — they simply never got beyond a single measurement.

**Third, and this is the one most often confused: "no taxon" does not mean "no structure."** The continuity of a latent variable cancels neither axes, nor profile, nor predictive power. It is only about the fact that boundaries between sorts of people are not found. Axes remain, differences remain, predictions work. What disappears is thresholds.

And a short caveat about what this part does **not** prove; I'll take it up in detail where I state the hypothesis. From "there is no boundary" it does not follow that "fuzzy representation wins." The continuity of a latent variable is perfectly compatible with an ordinary point estimate on a continuous scale — you can accept that there is no threshold and still describe a person with a single number. So this part does not prove the thesis of the essay. It removes a competing explanation and clears ground for the apparatus.

## 5. Fuzziness: The Right Form of Answer

**\[textbook\]**

Now the apparatus. In 1965 Lotfi Zadeh published a paper proposing something almost indecently simple: let membership in a set be a number between zero and one, rather than a "yes/no" answer.

That's it. That's the whole idea. A large discipline grew out of it afterward, but it starts here.

And here is the key turn that makes this part belong in the text. Partial membership is **not a measurement defect** to be removed one day by a precise test. It is the right form of answer for an object that has no sharp boundaries.

The difference is enormous. If you treat partial membership as a defect, then "0.6 in this category" reads as "we haven't yet determined whether it's yes or no." If you treat it as the right form, then "0.6" *is* the final answer — it will not get more precise, and it should not, because there is nothing more precise to say.

IQ imposed a sharp number on a fuzzy object. Hence the loss of information the first essay was about. Fuzzy logic legalizes what the series was already doing by hand.

**But on its own this claim stands on one leg, and the leg needs propping.**

Zadeh's formalism was invented in control theory. A reader has every right to ask: why does an apparatus developed for washing machines and elevators describe a person? Isn't this the import of pretty mathematics into someone else's field — precisely what I attacked astrologers for in the second essay?

There is an answer, and it comes from a different discipline, ten years before anyone spoke of personality. **Graded membership was found in human cognition experimentally.** Not postulated — found in data.

Three points have to be laid out in the right order here, because the order changes the force of the argument.

**The lead-in: the structure of a category is graded.** Inside a category there are "good" and "bad" members. A sparrow is a good bird, a penguin a bad one, though both are birds entirely. And this difference shows up already in perceptual encoding, not only in verbal report: across nine experiments with six hundred and sixty-three participants it appeared in reaction times, in the order of recall, in which example a person names first. This is about **typicality**.

**The carrying point: the boundaries of categories are blurred.** Thirty participants were shown five hundred and forty "item — category" pairs, and not once but twice, in two separate sessions. The result fell into three zones. Chair — furniture? Everyone agrees, and each person agrees with himself the second time. Cucumber — furniture? Everyone agrees it isn't, and again stably. But bookends — furniture? Here people disagree with each other — and, more importantly, **with themselves** from one session to the next. Blurriness not only between heads but inside one. This is about **membership**, and it is this point that carries my thesis.

**And a counter-current right away, which I am obliged to name myself** — for the same reason that below I will lay out two cases where fuzzy logic got caught.

Not everyone agrees that measured gradedness reflects gradedness of the representation itself. Armstrong, Gleitman and Gleitman made a move in 1983 that hurts: they gave people categories with **iron-clad definitions** to rate on a graded scale — even number, odd number, plane geometry figure. And they got the very same gradation: some odd numbers came out "more odd" than others. But oddness is strictly defined; there is no blurriness in it and cannot be.

And there's more. In the third experiment they first asked people directly: can membership in such a category be partial? People answered no — an odd number is either odd or it isn't. And then the same people were put through the same rating procedure, and they graded again. A person knows the category is strict, says so out loud — and still rates some numbers higher than others.

It looks as though part of the effect is induced by the procedure itself: what gets measured is the respondent's confidence rather than degree of membership. I do have an answer, but it came late and not from me — I'll return to it below.

And a second thing. Diesendruck and Gelman in 1999 gave forty-two participants nearly three hundred animals and artifacts and found a split by domain: about animals people judge **absolutely** — either a member of the category or not — while about man-made things they judge in degrees far more readily. So gradedness is stronger where the category was invented by humans and weaker where nature supposedly set it.

And since we're here, let me name a coincidence that would otherwise look like an oversight: **the study on which I just built my carrying point is about furniture.** Furniture is man-made, which by Diesendruck and Gelman makes it the most favorable class for me. My best piece of evidence was taken exactly where gradedness should show up most strongly. I admit it and don't hide it.

And since both objections are now on the table, it's worth saying that they are **not independent** — someone has checked whether the first explains the second. The check went through the respondent's confidence, the very thing Armstrong leans on. The outcome was partial: confidence does predict gradedness within each domain, but it does not explain the difference between animals and artifacts (Estes, 2004). That is, "it's just uncertainty" works in part, while the split by domain survives the correction for it. For me that is rather bad news: it means Diesendruck's finding doesn't dissolve into procedure, and has to be taken seriously.

**Both objections have received an answer, and neither answer is mine.** I wrote to James Hampton — the man whose distinction between typicality and membership holds this part up — and asked about the missing check. The reply came back better than the question.

**Armstrong first, and here it turns out his target was something else.** What they were after: it used to be thought that a typicality gradient proves a concept is prototype-structured. They showed the gradient appears for categories with iron-clad definitions too — so it doesn't prove that. The inference is invalid: a prototype predicts typicality, but typicality doesn't imply a prototype.

My argument makes no such inference. I separated typicality from membership explicitly, three paragraphs above, and my carrying point isn't about the gradient but about a person disagreeing with himself. So Armstrong's arrow flew past me — I just couldn't see it until it was explained to me.

**\[+math. 29.09\] And this is in print, not only in a letter.** In the same 1995 paper Hampton lists what else could account for differences in typicality besides prototype structure, and among them names "differences in frequency and familiarity amongst instances, for example in the case of some well-defined concepts" — citing Armstrong and colleagues precisely. The argument that was explained to me by letter had been lying in print for thirty-one years before I asked the question.

**And what's interesting in their result, on Hampton's reading, lies somewhere else entirely.** A person states outright that an odd number is a strict category, and then grades it anyway. Which means he doesn't know how his own concepts are built. Metacognitive access to one's own conceptual structure is poor. And notice where that lands: it is what my whole series is about. Philosophers — professionals at concepts — needed Wittgenstein to notice the vagueness of the terms they were setting out to define strictly.

**\[+psych. 29.09\] And here I acquired a support I did not have.** In that same 1995 paper Hampton ran four experiments to find necessary features in ordinary concepts — and **did not find them**. The test of the first prediction, he writes in the abstract, was rendered problematic as successive experiments failed to identify clearly necessary features for a range of concepts. His conclusion: it appears to be an unavoidable conclusion that naive subjects refuse to share the intuitions of the experts when it comes to concept definitions.

And then a sentence I would have put as the epigraph to the whole series, had it been mine:

> It is almost certainly a function of human civilisation (as embodied for example in science, philosophy, law making and civil administration) to take "folk" concepts and to render them more clearly defined. There will be a strong temptation for those with a great deal of education (such as researchers and thinkers concerned with concepts) to suppose that the degree of conceptual clarity that they have achieved themselves is also common to others. However this remains an empirical question and the failed attempt to find clearly defined concepts in the experiments reported here is evidence against that supposition.

The last sentence matters more than the first two: without it this is a graceful aside, with it a conclusion propped up by its author's own data. And it is not the same thing as poor metacognitive access but its mirror: not "a person doesn't know how his concepts are built" but "whoever has put his own house in order assumes the order is general." Said in print in 1995, and said by the very man who thirty years later explained the same thing to me by letter.

**Now the missing check — and it turns out it doesn't need to be run.** The answer lies in McCloskey and Glucksberg's data, and here's the uncomfortable part: it lies in the very sentence I wrote myself, three paragraphs above.

Read it again. Chair — agreement and stability. Cucumber — agreement and stability. Bookends — disagreement and inconsistency. I cited the three zones as an illustration of blurriness and failed to notice that they **discriminate** between two hypotheses.

Put it together: if the inconsistency were an artifact of the procedure, it would be spread across all levels of typicality. The procedure doesn't know whether an item is typical. It makes noise evenly everywhere.

It isn't spread evenly. It is concentrated exactly where the boundaries ought to be blurred.

**\[+psych. 29.09\] And this is not only my reading of their data.** Hampton wrote the same thing in print as early as 1995: people are inclined to disagree amongst themselves and to be inconsistent across occasions, "and this 'fuzziness' is largely restricted to the less typical instances of each category." The mechanism is named there too: variability in the placing of the category criterion and in the weight subjects attach to different features in different contexts.

**\[+math. 26.09\] And here a caveat is obligatory, or the argument claims more than it delivers — and picks a fight with my own source.** The logic above rules out **uniform** procedural noise, the kind independent of typicality. It does not rule out noise **correlated with the difficulty of the decision**: people answer at random where the decision is harder, and it is hardest on intermediate items. That is, the same profile is predicted by "uncertainty" as well, not only by a blurred concept. And this isn't an armchair quibble: Estes, whom I cite a paragraph below, showed that the respondent's confidence predicts gradedness within a domain. So the honest formulation is this: **the typicality profile excludes uniform noise but does not exclude noise correlated with difficulty.** The first is ruled out; the second stays open. **\[+math. 29.09\]** The printed source added above strengthens the **observation** — the blurriness really is concentrated on the less typical instances — but it does not close the caveat: the mechanism Hampton names there is an explanation compatible with the data, not a discrimination between two hypotheses.

"Five hundred and one is odd" is a limiting case of maximal agreement. So stability there should be expected to be nearly perfect — the more so since stability always runs higher than agreement.

The death condition doesn't disappear from this; it becomes a **named expectation**, and it is worth stating so that anyone could run the check. I have no absolute threshold and nowhere to get one: numbers out of thin air would be worse here than none. But there is a relative one, and it is testable within the same measurement that gave the three zones. On categories with iron-clad definitions, inconsistency must not exceed what that same measurement gives on high-typicality items — on the chair — and must sit closer to that than to the bookends. Let it come out at the bookends level, and the carrying point of this part falls.

**And in passing, something that had been bothering me gets resolved:** how stable differences between people coexist with instability within a person. They coexist because disagreement between people is larger than inconsistency within one — established, Hampton tells me, by Barsalou back in the eighties. And beyond that, something that begs to enter my own series: how inconsistent a person is turns out to be a stable individual trait in itself. Some people have sharp, stable category boundaries; others have blurred ones. Variability can itself be variable.

**And Diesendruck, for whom I had no reply.** Now there is one, and it comes from Hampton's threshold model: a sharp definition and graded typicality are compatible provided the probability of categorization rises monotonically with typicality.

Birds are a category with a sharp boundary. But that sharpness comes not from our diligence — nature supplied it: evolutionary divergence back in the age of dinosaurs left a gap. Intermediate categorization frequencies show up only on pterodactyls. Meanwhile typicality within birds varies enormously — sparrow versus penguin — and depends on which set of birds your culture takes for granted.

**\[+math. 29.09\]** This move is in print too. The 1995 paper says it outright: a category may appear to have no borderline cases "simply because no such cases occur in the natural world — BIRDS may be a case in point." The bird example is not from a letter.

So the stratification by domain is no puzzle. The only question is: **does the world supply a gap here.** Where it does, the threshold fires almost binary, even though typicality inside remains graded. Where it doesn't — and man-made things people produce as a continuous spectrum, from chair through stool to bookends — the threshold has nothing to grip.

By this measure a person is closer to the man-made than to birds: part four showed that boundaries between sorts of people were looked for and not found. No gap was found — so the threshold has nothing to grip.

**\[+math. 26.09, refined 29.09\]** A caveat on this explanation: it is Hampton's, not the study authors'. Diesendruck and Gelman explain the same stratification by **essentialism** — by people thinking of natural kinds as having a hidden essence. But they should not be made into rivals, and Hampton said so himself: his results, he writes, are consistent with the radical essentialism of Medin and Ortony, and more than that — essentialism predicts a **greater** degree of uncertainty in categorization than the classical model, because a person may know neither what the essence consists of nor whether the instance possesses it. So on the question I need — is membership graded — both theories pull the same way. They part company further on, in explaining why the domains differ; there I take Hampton's account, and say so plainly.

**And the last thing this correspondence gave — the strongest in the whole part.**

In the threshold model, as Hampton set it out in 1995, a prototype is a weighted sum of features with a threshold: each feature has its weight, there is a deciding threshold, then a decision. And if one set of features carries weight outweighing the sum of all the rest, and the threshold sits high enough, then those features become **defining**, and all the others characteristic.

He gives it with numbers. A concept of five features with weights 10, 10, 3, 2 and 1; the criterion, 20. Without either of the two heavy ones the maximum reachable is 16, short of the criterion: so each of them is necessary. With both, 20 is reached whatever the other features: so together they are sufficient. Two features out of five turn out to be both necessary and sufficient — defining in the exact classical sense. And a footnote there holds something still more elegant: drop the criterion to 10, and the very same concept takes on a **disjunctive** definition — the first feature or the second.

Consider what follows. Classical definition by necessary and sufficient features — the very thing that for two thousand years counted as the right way to fix a concept — turns out to be a **corner case** of the graded model. Not its competitor but a particular regime: the one you get at an extreme distribution of weights.

**\[+math. 29.09\] And immediately the condition, without which I would be claiming more than was given.** Hampton sets it down directly after this passage: the argument holds **only if the degree to which an instance possesses a feature is treated as all-or-none**. Allow partial possession, and the two models come apart again — and all four experiments of the paper are built on that very divergence. The irony is that partial possession is the subject of my essay. So the corner case is a formal result about the all-or-none case, and it cannot be carried over to graded features.

**\[+psych. 29.09\] And at once what honesty requires me to say alongside, or I shall be citing the paper against itself.** The linear summation of features is the version of the model that Hampton, in this very work, **tested and rejected**. The experiments came out against it: the effect of changing a feature was greatest not at the category boundary, as the linear scheme predicts, but where all the other features were positive. The paper's conclusion is that prototype theory is in need of revision; in its place two candidates are supported, the multiplicative rule of Medin and Shaffer and Shepard's exponential generalization function — and it is shown there that the two are effectively equivalent, since the exponential of a sum is a product of exponentials.

But the reduction to definitions survived it. In the corrected scheme it is restated: the scheme remains a prototype model so long as the threshold is strictly greater than zero, and with the threshold set at zero it reduces to the classical one — because the only way left to reject an instance is for it to lack a necessary feature. So the corner case does not depend on which function adds the features up. The author changed the mechanism and kept the conclusion.

**\[+math. 29.09\] The seam has to be marked here too, because the corner case rests on a stipulation the author puts in by hand.** Under a multiplicative rule, zeroing any feature zeroes the similarity — which would mean a poorly flying penguin stops being a bird. To prevent that, Hampton posits a constraint: only necessary features are allowed to fall to zero, the rest are bounded below. He calls the constraint arbitrary himself. And a second correction to the picture: necessary features do occur in prototype concepts, and often — all fruits are organic, all furniture has a human function. What collapses is not necessity but **sufficiency**: taken together, such features do not guarantee membership.

That is precisely the thesis of my essay, stated in someone else's field and in someone else's words. I claim that fuzziness is the right form of answer and that sharpness arises in the degenerate case. In part four it came out with types: categories turned out to be a corner case of the continuous. Here the same thing came out with definitions.

**\[+math. 29.09\]** And here it is tempting to say "two independent fields, one form of result" — which I will decline, because the results are of different kinds. In part four the corner case came out **on data**: categorical structure failed to replicate, continuous structure did. Here it came out **on paper**: it is a property of linear classifiers, not a fact about people. The second rhymes handsomely with the first and says the form of the answer was guessed right. But a rhyme is not a second measurement, and the two cannot be added into one sum. I wasn't looking for a third support — an echo arrived, and I am recording it as an echo.

**\[+math. 26.09\] Layer marking is obligatory here, because two things of unequal strength are stitched into one move.** That a linear classifier reduces to a conjunctive rule at an extreme distribution of weights is **\[textbook\]** — an elementary fact about linear classifiers, nothing to argue with. That human concepts are built as such a classifier is **\[framework\]**, and Hampton's framework, not mine. The classical view has its defenders, there are exemplar models, there are other explanations. I take this position as a strong argument with a name attached, not as an established fact.

**\[+psych. 29.09\] And a third line that was missing here.** The linear version itself is contested — by the author's own data, in the very paper this move is taken from. That does not break the marking, it confirms it: the text already said "I take this as a strong argument, not as an established fact," and here is the author revising that argument while keeping its conclusion. The corner case stayed, linearity went. That is exactly how a **\[framework\]** claim should behave, if the framework is alive.

**The distinction without which the first two points cannot be added together.** Typicality and graded membership are different things. "How good a member of the category is this" and "to what degree is it a member at all" are two different questions, and they do not reduce to each other. A penguin is an atypical bird, but a bird one hundred percent. A tomato, by contrast, is not a "bad vegetable," it is a disputed one, and its disputedness is of a different nature.

**\[+math. 29.09\]** The distinction is neither mine nor new. Hampton put it in print as early as 1995, and in the strongest form: there are many influences on rated typicality beyond how well an instance fits the concept representation — "to the point where typicality cannot be taken as a direct measure of degree of category membership." Twelve years later he took it apart at length in the separate paper I cite below.

The order here is a matter of substance, and I want to explain why I built it this way. The temptation is to put typicality first: it's better known, more striking, it has beautiful experiments. But then it would turn out that I warn against confusing typicality with membership and immediately stand on that very confusion: my thesis needs membership, but it is propped up by typicality. The first cognitive psychologist to come along would notice. So the weight is carried by the point about blurred boundaries, typicality serves as the lead-in, and the distinction stands as an explicit caveat.

> **Math inset 3. The membership function, and where the apparatus makes a refutable claim.** \[textbook\] A fuzzy set is given by a function **μ_A: X → [0,1]** assigning each object a degree of membership. Logical operations generalize: instead of "and" a **t-norm** **T(a,b)**, instead of "or" an **s-norm** **S(a,b)**. There isn't one of each — there are families: minimum, product, the Łukasiewicz operation, and so on. Here is what matters. From the t-norm axioms — monotonicity plus one as the neutral element — it follows in two lines that **T(a,b) ≤ T(a,1) = a** and **T(a,b) ≤ T(1,b) = b**, that is, **T(a,b) ≤ min(a,b) for every t-norm without exception**. The minimum is not one of the family's operations but its **upper bound**. Symmetrically, **S(a,b) ≥ max(a,b)**. And this is no longer a description of the apparatus but its signature under a testable promise: membership in the intersection of two concepts cannot exceed either membership separately, and no choice of operation within the family will change that. What happens when the promise is broken is in part seven.

**With this empirical foundation the logic of the part flips in my favor.**

Before: let's apply a fuzzy apparatus to a person, it fits.

After: human perception of humans **is already fuzzy** — that has been measured — and we spent a hundred years imposing sharp labels on it. At last the mathematics has arrived for what was happening anyway.

That also removes the charge of import. I did not bring fuzziness into psychology. It was already there.

## 6. Chaos: The Right Form of Forecast

**\[framework\]**

With the second kind of imprecision — the dynamical one — the story is shorter, because the first essay did most of the work. Here I only name the class.

The attractor, the Lyapunov exponent, the horizon of predictability. Since the trajectory cannot be computed in advance, you work with it differently: you don't predict a point, you describe the **shape** of the attractor — the region the system lives in — and separately you compute how far out a forecast makes sense at all. Chaos has its own honest science. It is about the geometry of the possible, not about predicting the particular.

> **Math inset 4. The Lyapunov exponent and the horizon of predictability.** \[textbook\] Take two nearly identical starting points differing by **δ₀**. In a chaotic system the distance between their trajectories grows roughly as **|δ(t)| ≈ |δ₀| · e^(λt)**, where **λ > 0** is the Lyapunov exponent. A forecast stops making sense when the divergence reaches an acceptable **Δ**. Hence the horizon: **t_hor ≈ (1/λ) · ln(Δ / |δ₀|)**. The logarithm here isn't taken from a textbook, it comes out in one line: set **|δ₀| · e^(λt)** equal to the tolerance **Δ** and solve for **t**. The logarithm is the inverse of the exponential, and since the error grows exponentially, the time until it exceeds tolerance grows logarithmically. That is exactly why the dependence is so flat rather than linear. And hidden in this formula is the strongest consequence in the whole essay, easy to notice only if you compute it. **The dependence of the horizon on precision is logarithmic.** Take **λ = 0.5** and a tolerance of one. At an initial precision of **10⁻²** the horizon equals 9.2. Improve precision **a millionfold**, to **10⁻⁸** — and the horizon becomes 36.8. That is, it grows **fourfold**. A million to four. If you want the rule in one phrase, here it is: to **double** the horizon you must **square** the precision. Investing in instruments is nearly useless not because the instruments are bad but because the logarithm grows slowly. This is the answer to the precision camp in its own language: your road isn't closed, it simply doesn't lead there — the limit doesn't stand where you're digging.

**Now an honest problem I should name myself.**

I promised that the object carries **both** kinds of imprecision. But look at how the empirical support is distributed. Parts four and five are propped up by measurements — taxometrics, experiments with categories. And this part is entirely carried over from the first essay, without a single piece of evidence of its own. Half the promise is backed, half is not.

This is closed by a single anchor, and it fits almost perfectly.

**\[textbook\]**

In 2001 William Fleeson published a study in which people were measured not with a single questionnaire but with dozens of samples over weeks, in natural conditions. It turned out that the spread of behavior **within one person** over time is comparable to the spread between different people. One and the same person on different days differs from himself about as much as different people differ from one another.

Hence the formulation I need: a person is not a point on a trait scale but a **distribution of states**. Later he put it even more cleanly: traits predict momentary behavior poorly and the average over a long stretch well.

What this gives, in three points.

First: **a floor under the claim "a distribution instead of a point,"** which until now had none — it rested on a single line of reasoning.

Second: **psychological evidence for the dynamical side.** This is not chaos in the technical sense — nobody measured a Lyapunov exponent here, and I won't pretend otherwise. But it is a measured fact that describing a person with a single value erases the bulk of his spread. The dynamical side stops being a carry-over from the previous text.

Third: **a link to the third essay coming from the other end.** There the irreducible residual was explained by averaging killing the if-then pattern: a person who does one thing in some conditions and another in others turns, under averaging, into a pale middle that exists in no conditions at all. Here the same thing is visible from the data side — the averaged value discards a spread comparable to the spread between people.

**And fourth — it settles a question that by this point should already have arisen.**

In part four I showed that there are no castes, only continuous axes. Here I say a person is not a point but a distribution. So which is he: a point on an axis or a cloud of points?

The answer: one is a **collapse** of the other, and there is no confusion once you lay out three levels in order.

First: **there are no sorts.** Taxometrics found no threshold dividing people into two kinds — that's part four.

Second: **there are axes.** Nobody proved their absence, and I don't either. Sociability as a dimension hasn't gone anywhere.

Third, and this one is new here: **a score on an axis is no longer an observation but the result of an operation.** The number a person receives on a trait scale is a summary of his distribution of states, usually simply its mean. There was a cloud, and the number is what remained of the cloud after collapse.

From this you can see where this text strikes and where it doesn't. Not at the axes — the axes are fine. At the **collapse**. The argument is not about whether an axis of sociability exists, but about what is lost when a person on that axis is written down as one number. Part four removed the castes and left the axes; this part says that even on the axes that remain, a point is not the whole answer.

**And a caveat right away, which I am obliged to make so as not to commit the error I warned against in part three.** This study is about **distributions**, that is, about the probabilistic side, not about fuzziness. The spread of behavior over time and the blurriness of a category's boundaries are different things, and I set them side by side, not into one. This anchor works as a bridge between the dynamical side and the next inset, not as a prop for fuzzy logic.

> **Math inset 5. A distribution instead of a point.** \[framework\] If the object is not a point, then an honest answer about it is not a point. Let a person's behavior on some axis at moment **t** be **X_t**. The classical description issues one number: **x̄ = E[X_t]**, the mean. The question worth asking: how much information has been thrown away? The answer — everything except the first moment: all the variance, all the skew, all the shape. And if the spread within a person is comparable to the spread between people, then what was discarded is comparable in size to what was kept. An honest description is the distribution **p(X)** itself: where the center is, how wide it is, whether there are two humps instead of one. Two humps, incidentally, is the most interesting case: a person who behaves one way in some conditions and differently in others yields, under averaging, a middle he is never in. That is the if-then pattern from the first essay, written in the language of distributions.

## 7. The Main Move, and the Inoculation Immediately

Now the main claim, the one this whole text was written for.

**All the instruments listed are united by a single refusal: not to eliminate uncertainty but to work with it.**

The classical approach treats imprecision as an enemy. Enemies are crushed: a finer instrument, a larger sample, a better test — and imprecision retreats. The soft approach treats imprecision as a **property of the object**, to be represented honestly rather than erased. If the object has no boundaries, then writing "no boundaries" *is* the correct entry, not an interim one.

And here I am obliged, immediately, in this same part, to administer an inoculation. Because the sentence above is exactly what the popular books say. "Embrace uncertainty," "work with chaos," "stop demanding precise answers of yourself." The same words.

What saves it is operationality, and it is there. Membership functions that compute. T-norms instead of "and," s-norms instead of "or." Inference rules that yield an unambiguous result. And working devices: as early as 1975 Mamdani and Assilian assembled a fuzzy controller for a laboratory steam engine — rules stated by an operator in ordinary words were turned into an automatic control strategy, and it worked better than expected.

The formulation worth remembering: **soft methods are not softness of thinking but hard mathematics of imprecise quantities.** The same boundary that in the second essay ran between the symbol-as-generator and the symbol-as-instrument.

But declarations aren't enough. So what follows is material evidence, and I lay it out myself, first, the way the third essay laid out the theorem that struck at me.

**The case where fuzzy logic got caught.**

It's known as **the guppy problem**. In 1981 Osherson and Smith showed the following. Fuzzy conjunction requires that membership in the intersection of two concepts not exceed membership in either of them separately — that is the very inequality from inset three.

And the experiment gives the opposite. A guppy is rated by people as an **excellent** member of the concept "pet fish." Yet a guppy is not a typical "pet" — not a dog, not a cat. And it is not a typical "fish" either — not a cod, not a pike. Membership in the intersection turned out higher than membership in both constituents.

The formalism made a quantitative prediction about human judgments — and missed. The polemic around this spot has been running for forty years: Zadeh himself replied, others argued against him and for him, and the dispute continues.

**And here is a subtlety usually skipped over, which reverses the conclusion.**

The temptation is to say: fine, the minimum didn't fit, let's take another operation from the family, there are plenty. Well — **it won't help**. The inequality **T(a,b) ≤ min(a,b)** holds not for the minimum but for **all** t-norms at once; the minimum is the family's upper bound. And the guppy effect yields a value above even the maximum. No operation in the class can produce that, and the trouble is not an unlucky choice.

This is repaired not by replacing the operation but by abandoning a structural assumption: membership in a compound concept ceases to be computed from the memberships of its constituents. That assumption has a name — **truth-functionality** — and it means exactly what it says: the value of the whole is a function of the values of the parts. That is what falls. Not an operation inside it, but it itself.

**And here is why I think this case works for me rather than against me.**

Compare two formulations. "The formalism erred, then a parameter was tweaked" — that sounds like curve-fitting, and the opponent on the left will read it that way. And: "a whole family of operations made the prediction, a whole family missed, and the repair required throwing out a structural assumption" — that is no longer curve-fitting. That is science working.

The apparatus forbade something that no variant of it could have permitted. The prohibition was violated. The apparatus had to be rebuilt.

**And fog cannot do that.** "Embrace your ambiguity" cannot be refuted by an experiment with a small fish. That claim forbids nothing at all, which means no experiment can contradict it. Fuzzy logic forbade, got caught, and was rebuilt. The opponent on the left receives not an argument but material evidence: **this is what a formalism that can turn out to be wrong looks like, and this is how it differs from one that cannot.**

**The boundary of the damage, so I don't overstate in the other direction.** The attack by Osherson and Smith lands on **truth-functional combination of concepts** — on the assumption that membership in a compound is computed from the memberships of the parts. My thesis needs no combination at all: it needs the one-place graded membership of a person in an archetype. Acknowledging the problem explicitly is necessary. Inflating it is not.

**Now the second hole, and it is in the load-bearing spot. I'll name this one myself too.**

Above I defended soft methods by pointing to working devices: fuzzy control is not a metaphor, it computes and turns real iron from the mid-seventies onward. The argument looks convincing.

It won't do. The best-known critique of fuzzy logic — Elkan's 1993 paper — consists of two observations, and the second strikes right here: **the practical success of fuzzy logic falls almost entirely on embedded controllers, whereas the theoretical work concerns knowledge representation and reasoning, and the success of the first does not transfer to the second.**

And my application — describing a person — is representation, not control. Which means I am defending representation by pointing to controllers, and that is exactly the step Elkan calls unwarranted. A well-read reader would have removed that paragraph with a single reference.

**It is repaired by narrowing the claim — and after the repair the argument gets stronger, not weaker.**

Controllers do not prove that the apparatus is suitable for describing people. That they genuinely do not prove, and I won't pretend otherwise any longer. They prove something else: that the apparatus is **capable at all of yielding testable predictions, of getting caught, and of being repaired**. And that is the boundary between mathematics and fog, which is what this whole part was written for.

Then the inoculation has two pieces of material evidence instead of one shaky piece, and both are about refutability. The guppy: a prediction about human judgments that missed. Controllers: predictions about hardware that land, but in a different domain. Neither is passed off as a transfer of success to my problem.

The formulation I want to reduce this to: **what I take from fuzzy logic is not its track record but its capacity to be wrong in a testable way.**

## 8. Hypothesis 7 and How to Kill It

The series numbers its hypotheses continuously: the first essay advanced hypotheses one through three, the second, four and five, the third, six in two variants. This is the seventh.

**Decided 23 August 2026 by the author: the soft variant.** The accompanying predictions are taken out of the body of the hypothesis and stand beside it, testable separately. The justification and the price are below, after the death conditions.

> **Hypothesis 7.** On those axes of personality description where the series has proved irreducible imprecision, a fuzzy representation — partial membership, a distribution instead of a point — yields a reproducible predictive advantage over a sharp one: over a category and over a single value, **and that answer is operational** — it changes the actions of whoever uses it.

The claim is compound, and both halves are load-bearing. The first is about data: does the fuzzy representation win in a measurement. The second is about applicability: does that advantage do anything at all outside the table. The "and" is placed there deliberately, and below it will have a direct consequence.

> **Accompanying prediction (a).** The advantage grows the stronger the if-then pattern is in a person: the more his behavior depends on conditions, the more a sharp description loses.
>
> **Accompanying prediction (b).** The advantage grows with the length of the prediction horizon: the further ahead, the more noticeable the superiority of a fuzzy representation.
>
> Both predictions **are not part of the hypothesis** and their failure is not its refutation. The separation is fixed here, **before any measurement** — see the caveat about hindsight below.

**How to kill it.** Two conditions, and **any one** suffices — not both at once. This follows directly from the content being compound: for an "and" to become false, refuting one half is enough. By joining the two parts with "and" I have made the hypothesis twice as vulnerable myself — and that is not a slip but a price paid deliberately.

- **Metric.** If **on those very axes where the series has proved irreducible imprecision** a sharp metric yields a reproducible advantage over a fuzzy one, the hypothesis is dead: it means the object is sharp there too, and the soft method is superfluous.
- **Operational, for the first person.** If a soft answer about yourself changes not a single one of a person's actions, then it is not an instrument but a consolation, and **the hypothesis is dead** regardless of any statistics: its second half is false, and a compound claim does not survive that. Not even a brilliant result on the first condition saves it here: the fuzzy representation may win on data as much as you like and still fail to clear this threshold.

The caveat about scope is not a dodge, and I'll explain what it's for. The hypothesis asserts an advantage on delimited axes, so testing outside those axes tests a different claim — not mine. The thesis is not "everything is fuzzy" but "here is a criterion, and here is the domain where it applies."

The first-person condition was added after a remark of 2 August and it matters more to me than the first. It leaves no room to hide behind "but it's more honest." Honesty that changes no behavior is no different from a pretty label — and the whole second essay was written against pretty labels.

**What happens if the accompanying predictions are not confirmed.** Not death but narrowing — and I say in advance which narrowing, so there are no excuses later.

**A failure of (a)** would mean that the advantage of fuzzy representation is not tied to a person's internal variability but comes from somewhere else. That tears the link to the first and third essays: there the irreducible residual was explained precisely by the if-then pattern. The core stands, but the series stops being one line and becomes four texts side by side.

**A failure of (b)** would mean the advantage does not accumulate over time: a fuzzy representation describes better but does not predict further ahead better. The core stands, but my main argument against the precision camp subsides badly — "your instruments will hit the horizon" stops working.

**Now about the decision to take (a) and (b) outside, and about its price.**

Two different virtues collide here, and I want to name them out loud rather than smooth them over.

**Refutability** says: the easier a theory is to kill, the more honest it is. By that measure it would have been better to pull both predictions inside the hypothesis — then any miss fells everything, and there is nowhere to retreat.

**Antifragility** says something else: what matters is not only dying from a blow but becoming more precise from a blow that didn't kill. And by that measure the hard variant turns out to be outright bad. In it there are only two outcomes: "all correct" or "all dead," there is no third. Such a construction gains nothing from partial refutation — it either stands whole or collapses whole. The soft variant extracts knowledge from the very same measurement: a failure of (a) or (b) doesn't fell the core but shows **what exactly** the core rests on, and the next version of the hypothesis comes out more precise than the previous one.

**There is a price, and I acknowledge it directly: this way the hypothesis is harder to kill than in the hard variant.** By the series' own criterion that is a minus, and I won't pretend it isn't there.

It is offset by three things, all three obligatory.

First: **the predictions are not deleted but moved out.** They remain stated, testable, and will receive their own verdict. A claim that can be shown false does honest work even when its failure doesn't fell the parent thesis.

Second: **the separation is fixed in advance.** The danger of the soft variant is not the division into main and secondary but division **in hindsight** — when something doesn't come out, declaring it secondary. Here the order is reversed: the separation was written down before anyone measured anything, and written into a public repository with dates. Anyone can check.

Third: **each failure has its own named damage**, and it is named above. Without that an "accompanying prediction" would turn into a universal excuse.

The difference from the third essay's case, so they aren't conflated. There it was two **co-equal** hypotheses, and the durability arose by itself, in hindsight — it had to be admitted as a defect. Here the subordination is declared in advance and together with its price. The difference is not in durability but in whether it was declared before the measurement or discovered after.

**And the obligatory distinction — it protects the hypothesis from what you've already read.**

You remember part four: the taxometric criterion has already been applied to enormous samples, and it mostly answered in my favor. So the question will come immediately: are you advancing a hypothesis whose answer you yourself showed four parts ago?

I'll answer before the question. **These are three different questions, not one.**

Taxometrics answered the question **"is there a boundary"**: do people divide along a given axis into two sorts with a real threshold? The answer on most axes tested is no, the difference is one of degree.

Hypothesis 7 answers the question **"does it yield an advantage"**: does a fuzzy representation improve prediction compared with a sharp one?

And the accompanying predictions answer a third question — **"does the advantage grow"**, and with what.

From "there is no boundary" it does not follow that "fuzzy representation wins": the continuity of a latent variable is compatible with an ordinary point estimate on a continuous scale. And for the same reason, from "there is a boundary" it does not follow that "a sharp metric wins." The presence or absence of a threshold is a question about the object's construction. The predictive superiority of a representation is a question of comparing two models on data. The second does not follow from the first in either direction.

So part four does not confirm the hypothesis. It clears ground for it, removing the competing explanation "you simply searched for the category badly."

## 9. How This Connects to the Third Essay and the Series

**\[framework\]**

The third essay established that intelligence is object and function at once, and that identity here is **extensional**: two intelligences count as one if they behave identically. Not "are built identically" — behave. You cannot check the construction; you can check the behavior.

But there is a caveat there that I skipped past at the time, and now it turns out to be central. Identity by behavior works not "exactly" but **"up to."** Up to a permutation, up to a rescaling, up to the observed sample. Every time we say two behaviors are the same, we mean the same within something.

And "up to" is precisely a fuzzy equivalence relation.

> **Math inset 6. A fuzzy equivalence relation.** \[textbook\] Ordinary equivalence answers "yes" or "no": **x ~ y** either holds or doesn't, and out of it come disjoint classes. The fuzzy version replaces the answer with a degree **μ_R(x,y) ∈ [0,1]** and requires three properties: **reflexivity** **μ_R(x,x) = 1** (fully equal to itself), **symmetry** **μ_R(x,y) = μ_R(y,x)**, and **T-transitivity** **μ_R(x,z) ≥ T(μ_R(x,y), μ_R(y,z))** — if **x** resembles **y** and **y** resembles **z**, then **x** resembles **z** by no less than "both together" under the chosen t-norm. The last property is weaker than ordinary transitivity, and that is the whole point: resemblance can **accumulate losses** along a chain. Classes here don't fall apart into disjoint boxes, they flow into one another. That is exactly what happens with "identical behavior": two intelligences coincide at 0.9, the second with the third at 0.9, and the first with the third perhaps already at 0.8.

From this follows a claim stronger than the one I started this part for. The object-function does not merely **require** fuzzy instruments because we observe it badly. It is fuzzy **in its own definition**. The very notion of "the same intelligence" is graded, not two-valued.

And a second link, the most direct of all.

The third essay spoke of structural loss under compression: when a person is folded into a single value, what is lost is not precision but structure, and the loss is not recoverable. At the time that was reasoning. In fuzzy logic this operation has a name, standard algorithms, and a one-line proof of the loss.

> **Math inset 7. Defuzzification and its price.** \[textbook\] Defuzzification is the conversion of a fuzzy answer back into a single number. The standard method is the center of gravity: **x\* = ∫ x·μ(x) dx / ∫ μ(x) dx**. The operation is necessary: in the end you have to do something, and an action is usually singular. But look at it as a mapping **μ ↦ x\***. It sends an entire function to one number, and therefore it is **not injective**: infinitely many different membership functions give the same center of gravity. A narrow peak at 5, and two distant humps symmetric about 5, both give **x\* = 5**. So recovering **μ** from **x\*** is impossible — not "difficult," impossible, and it follows directly from non-injectivity. That is the whole price: the loss of information here is **proved, not asserted**. The third essay called this structural loss under compression; here it has a formula, an algorithm, and a proof. The practical conclusion: defuzzify **as late as possible** — compute in fuzzy form until the last step, and collapse to a number only at the moment of action. Every premature defuzzification throws away structure that cannot afterward be recovered. And one last thing worth carrying in mind since part six: **a score on a trait scale is built the same way.** A distribution of states is folded into a mean by an integral of the same form, with the same non-injectivity and the same irreversibility. The objects differ, and they must not be confused: a membership function is not normalized, a probability density is, and those are two different things from part three. What is identical is the **operation** — collapsing a function into a number — and therefore so is its bill.

**And an important caveat about this text's place in the series.** It does not put a full stop. After it there is another text in the queue — about the game of two intelligences, each trying to read the other while knowing the other is doing the same. This text **supplies the instrument** for what the next one will run into. The full stop is put by the next one.

## 10. Objections

Prepared in advance, not after the draft.

**"Fuzzy logic alone won't describe a whole person."** Fair, and I don't claim it will. The thesis is not about Zadeh's single apparatus but about the whole class of soft computing. Fuzzy logic is the flagship example, not the only instrument; alongside it stand probabilistic and dynamical methods, and in this text all three were needed.

**"This is just ordinary statistics in new packaging."** No, and the difference is checkable by addition. Probability is about not knowing an outcome when the boundaries of the event are sharp: you don't know whether it will land heads or tails, but what counts as "heads" is defined absolutely precisely. Fuzziness is about the blurriness of the boundaries themselves: what counts as "a tall person" is not defined, and no amount of observation will define it. Formally: for probabilities the sum over alternatives equals one, for memberships it is free. Confusing them is a common error, and in this text I tried not to commit it; inset two shows where the seam runs.

**"A fuzzy answer gives nothing in practice."** Agreed — which is why I wrote it directly into the hypothesis's death condition. If a soft answer about yourself changes not a single action, the thesis is dead in that part. The objection isn't parried; it's converted into a criterion.

**"This is the same esoterica, only with references."** The answer is the whole of part seven. Esoterica cannot be wrong: there is no experiment that would refute "embrace your ambiguity." Fuzzy logic made a quantitative prediction about human judgments, missed on a guppy, and had to be rebuilt. The capacity to be wrong in a testable way — that is the difference.

**"The link between prototypes and fuzzy sets was refuted long ago."** This will come from a well-read reader, and he'll be half right. What was refuted is the link with **the combination of concepts** — I laid that case out myself, in part seven, ahead of any objections. My thesis doesn't need combination: it needs one-place graded membership. And I'll repeat what matters most to me: the refutability of the apparatus is my argument, not my problem.

**"You say everything is continuous, but some categories are real."** They are, and part four admits it in plain text, with numbers. My thesis is comparative, not absolute: in most of the cases tested, no boundary was found. The same move as in the second essay, where the argument was about a proportion, not about zero.

**"Your apparatus was invented for washing machines."** Invented there, true. But graded membership was found in human cognition experimentally and independently — ten years before anyone spoke of a fuzzy description of personality. I did not bring fuzziness into psychology. And separately: I do **not** transfer the success of controllers to describing people — Elkan showed such a transfer is illegitimate, and I agree with him. Controllers prove only the refutability of the apparatus, and nothing more.

**And the self-criticism better spoken by me.** There are two layers here, and they compound.

**Layer one: there is no measurement of my own in this text.** I brought methods together but showed not a single measurement conducted by me on a person. Other people's measurements did appear — taxometrics, experiments with categories, the study on distributions of states — but they are about something else: they answer the question "is there a boundary," not the question of hypothesis 7.

**Layer two, and it arose from my own decision.** I moved the accompanying predictions outside, beyond the hypothesis. From the outside it looks like this: a man advanced a claim, arranged in advance that two-thirds of the construction cannot count as its refutation, and measured nothing. It costs a reader nothing to assemble that chain himself, and in his rendition it will sound like a prepared retreat.

So I assemble it first. **This text has two honest weaknesses, and they reinforce each other.** There is no measurement. The structure of the hypothesis was chosen to be durable. Each on its own is tolerable; together they give exactly the look in which theories become unfalsifiable.

What I can say against myself. The durability was not bought for free, and the price is written down. The separation into core and accompanying was fixed **before** any measurement, not after a failed one. The predictions haven't vanished — they are stated and awaiting their verdict. Each possible failure has a concrete damage named in advance: what exactly in the picture would turn out to be wrong. And all of it lies in a public repository with dates, so anyone can check the order.

The difference between honest durability and dishonest durability lies entirely in the order. Here the order is documented — but I agree that this is weaker than a conducted experiment, and I don't want the one taken for the other.

## 11. The Return

Let's go back to the green "Great."

It's still sitting there as a file called `category.png`. The system announced that it now knows who I am. It knows exactly one thing: which checkbox I ticked when I was made to tick one. Everything else I knew about myself at that moment never made it into the database — not because it was hidden, but because there was no field for it.

Now that scene has names, and all of them come from this text. The forty entries in the list are a sharp partition of an object in which no boundaries were found. The demand to pick one is defuzzification, and at the earliest step, where losing structure costs the most. And my freezing in front of the form was not confusion. It was the right response to a wrong form of answer: I stalled precisely because an honest quantity would not fit into the field it was being put in.

The order here matters, and it was set back in part two: **the question about yourself is settled first, and only from there does it transfer to another person.**

About yourself. The form that was missing from the dropdown does exist in language — only as hedges: "depends when," "mostly," "varies." Hedges sound like weakness, because we're used to counting the unambiguous answer as the strong one. But if the object has no boundaries, then an unambiguous answer is not strength but loss: it throws away structure that cannot afterward be recovered. And that loss, as it turns out, has a formula.

Now about another person. The first essay claimed you cannot finish reading a person. Three texts later I'm not about to cancel that — it was true and remains true. But it has acquired a continuation it didn't have before.

You cannot finish reading. **You can work honestly.**

The difference between these two statements is not one of mood but of having an instrument. "You cannot finish reading," with no continuation, is either grounds for despair or grounds for giving up trying, and both are bad. With a continuation it becomes a technical remark: you are working with an object that has no sharp boundaries and no predictable trajectory — here is the class of methods designed for exactly such an object, here is what they can do, here is what they cannot, and here are the results at which I will admit I was wrong.

And the last thing, the reason all of this was written. If it seems to you that a fuzzy answer about yourself is permission not to bother, it's the other way around. Permission not to bother is what a sharp label issues: name a type, and there's nothing further to think about. A fuzzy answer demands more work, not less. And the work is concrete — every item in it has a formula in this text.

**Know on which axes you are no judge of yourself.** That's **w(axis)**: your band is wider where a trait is more heavily loaded with evaluation. Practically — on such axes, ask your friends rather than yourself. And notice that the practical advice is firmer than its grounding: the function **w(axis)** is my inference and nobody's measurement, whereas "on evaluatively loaded traits your friends are more accurate than you" is measured — that is Vazire, from part two.

**Know that you are a distribution, not a point.** That means being able to name not only the center but the spread. And separately, to check whether you have one hump or two. Because if there are two, then your mean describes a state you are never in: a person who does one thing in some conditions and another in others turns, under averaging, into a middle that exists under no conditions at all.

**Know the price of a checkbox.** Every time you answer about yourself in one word, you perform a defuzzification. Sometimes it's necessary — you have to act unambiguously. But now you have the bill: you are giving up structure that cannot be recovered from the number, and therefore the collapse is worth doing at the last step rather than the first.

That is harder than being a type. But it's true.

## Formulas

The whole essay in the language of formulas — part by part. There are four carrying symbols: `μ_A(x) ∈ [0,1]` — the degree of membership of object `x` in category `A`; `θ` — the object and `f` — the function, both carried over from the third essay unchanged; `λ` — the Lyapunov exponent; `w(axis)` — the width of the band of uncertainty as a function of the axis of description. Layer tags (\[textbook\] / \[framework\] / \[hypothesis\]) are preserved.

**1. The Thesis** · \[framework\]

- `answer = μ_A(x) ∈ [0,1]`, not `μ_A(x) ∈ {0,1}` — The whole change of instrument in one line: the set of admissible answers widens from two points to an interval. Not "we don't yet know whether it's zero or one," but "the right answer lies in between."
- `imprecision = property(object)`, not `property(instrument)` — The carrying claim. The precision camp reads imprecision as temporary; the text claims it is in the object.

**2. The Fork: Two Sources of Imprecision** · \[textbook\]

- chaos: `|δ(t)| ≈ |δ₀| · e^(λt)`, `λ > 0` — The rule is sharp, the trajectories diverge. A small difference in initial conditions grows exponentially.
- fuzziness: `μ_A: X → [0,1]` — The rule is blurred, the boundaries partial. What diverges is not trajectories but membership itself.
- `∑ᵢ p(Aᵢ) = 1` versus `∑ᵢ μ_{Aᵢ}(x)` — free — The formal answer to "this is just ordinary statistics." Probability lives on a simplex: the shares divide one. Memberships carry no such budget. The difference is not philosophical but checkable by addition.
- `∑ μ = 1` (essay 2) → `∑ μ` free (essay 4) — The passage from the second essay to the fourth is the removal of normalization. "A person is distributed among castes" becomes "a person partially belongs to each."

**3. A Check Before the Apparatus: Taxometrics** · \[textbook\]

- `60/92 = 65.2%` → `4/102 = 3.9%` — The proportion of studies declaring types: without a check against simulated data versus with one. The check is two-sided — the data are compared both with a categorical model and with a continuous one — so a dimensional outcome is a choice, not an empty result. As fractions, not percentages — they weigh more.
- `38.9%` versus `14%` — **The 2012 review.** Two numbers of different natures, and neither is a direct observation of categories: the first is the proportion of findings that **the original authors judged** taxonic, the second the output of a model after statistical control of confounds. Not to be set side by side without a marker.
- `16.7%` (53/317) versus `57.8%` (107/185) — **The 2020 meta-analysis.** Two observed proportions: in studies with a comparative fit index and without one. No model involved, and so this pair is firmer than the previous one.
- `4.88×` — **Not the ratio of those proportions.** An odds ratio from a regression controlling for construct domain. The observed ratio of proportions is `57.8 / 16.7 = 3.46`; one cannot be derived from the other.
- `precision(procedure) ↑ ⟹ share(taxa) ↓` — The reversal for whose sake this part stands where it does. Usually the finer the instrument, the more structure shows; here the opposite. Hence `sharpness ∈ properties(the look)`, not `properties(the object)`.

**4. Fuzziness: The Right Form of Answer** · \[textbook\]

- `μ_A: X → [0,1]` (Zadeh, 1965) — The definition. Everything else in the part is a consequence.
- `T(a,b) ≤ min(a,b) ≤ max(a,b) ≤ S(a,b)` — The bounds of the **family**, not properties of a particular operation. From the axioms: `T(a,b) ≤ T(a,1) = a` and `T(a,b) ≤ T(1,b) = b`. The minimum is the upper bound of all t-norms, the maximum the lower bound of all s-norms.
- the choice of `T` within the family is a matter of experiment — An independent claim about calibration. **Not** connected to the guppy problem: see block 6.

**5. Chaos: The Right Form of Forecast** · \[textbook\] + \[framework\]

- `t_hor ≈ (1/λ) · ln(Δ / |δ₀|)` — The horizon of predictability at tolerance `Δ` and initial imprecision `δ₀`. \[textbook\]
- the dependence on precision is **logarithmic** — The strongest consequence. At `λ = 0.5` and a tolerance of one, improving initial precision a millionfold lengthens the horizon fourfold: from 9.2 to 36.8. To double the horizon you must square the precision. Investing in instruments is nearly useless — the limit does not stand there. \[textbook\]
- `x̄ = E[X_t]` discards everything but the first moment — If the spread within a person is comparable to the spread between people, then what is discarded is comparable to what is kept. \[framework\]

**6. The Main Move and the Inoculation** · \[framework\]

- `not eliminate(H)`, but `represent(H)` — The refusal that unites the whole class of instruments.
- the guppy: `μ(pet fish) > max(μ(pet), μ(fish))` — The observed violation. **No t-norm** yields the required value: the family's ceiling is the minimum, and here even the maximum is exceeded.
- conclusion: what falls is `truth-functionality`, not the choice of `T` — The repair required abandoning a structural assumption, not tweaking a parameter. That is the whole difference between mathematics and fog.

**7. Hypothesis 7** · \[hypothesis\]

- `H = C₀ ∧ O` — The content of the hypothesis is a conjunction: the advantage of fuzzy representation on the delimited axes **and** the operationality of the answer in the first person. Both halves are named in the statement of the hypothesis in part 8 as well — formula and prose say the same thing.
- `death(H) = ¬C₀ ∨ ¬O` — The death conditions are a disjunction: any one suffices.
- `¬(A ∧ B) = ¬A ∨ ¬B` — De Morgan's rule. It explains why the "and" of the content turns into an "or" in the death conditions.
- `Cₐ, C_b` — **beside, not inside**: `¬Cₐ ⟹ narrowing(H)`, not `¬H` — The accompanying predictions are taken out of the body of the hypothesis: their failure does not fell the core but makes precise what the core rests on. The arrow leads to "narrowing," not to "false."
- `fixed(separation) < measured(anything)` — The condition without which the previous line becomes an excuse: the separation was recorded **earlier** than any measurement.
- `w = w(axis)`, grows with the evaluativeness of the axis — The band of uncertainty of self-assessment is not constant. \[framework\]

**8. The Link to the Third Essay** · \[textbook\] + \[framework\]

- `μ_R(x,x) = 1`; `μ_R(x,y) = μ_R(y,x)`; `μ_R(x,z) ≥ T(μ_R(x,y), μ_R(y,z))` — A fuzzy equivalence relation. The formalization of "up to." \[textbook\]
- `f₁ ≡ f₂` (essay 3, sharp) → `μ_R(f₁,f₂) ∈ [0,1]` (essay 4) — The identity of intelligence in the third essay was two-valued. Here it becomes visible that it too is graded: the object-function is fuzzy **in its own definition**. \[framework\]

**9. The Emblem: Defuzzification and Its Price**

- `x* = ∫ x·μ(x) dx / ∫ μ(x) dx` — The conversion of a fuzzy answer back into a single number.
- the mapping `μ ↦ x*` is **not injective** — Infinitely many different membership functions give the same center of gravity ⟹ `μ` cannot be recovered from `x*`. The loss of information here is **proved, not asserted**.
- `defuzzification = compression with loss of structure` — What the third essay called structural loss under compression, now with a name, an algorithm and a proof. The practical conclusion: defuzzify as late as possible.

***\[+math. 29.09, from the critical reader's review\] Six further edits, five of them narrowing, one strengthening.***

*The strengthening one, and the most important. The instrument in part four had been described wrongly — as a check for false positives: "whether the method finds types where types are known not to exist." Under that description, "no boundary was found" is an absence of evidence, and the objection "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" lands squarely. But the comparative fit index compares the data against simulated data of **two kinds**, categorical and continuous, and asks which they resemble more. A dimensional outcome is a choice between two models, not an empty result. The description has been corrected, and the 2020 meta-analysis has gained its own formulation: dimensional findings outnumber taxonic ones five to one.*

*The narrowing ones. The defence of the method through biological sex has been brought down to what it actually gives: the procedure is not blind, but sex is a taxon of enormous separation, and sensitivity to a taxon of modest size does not follow. The question of power is named as open; this was my own edit of 29.09, and it fell to me to narrow it. In part five the flat "there is no gap" became "no gap was found." The register of the linear-classifier move was lowered: in part four the corner case came out on data, here on paper, and the second is an echo of the first rather than a second measurement. And in the closing it is noted that the advice to ask one's friends is firmer than its grounding: **w(axis)** is my inference, while the asymmetry of accuracy is measured.*

*One thing came free: the objection "the literature is clinical, so it doesn't transfer to personality" is disposed of by the 2020 abstract, which states that psychopathology was no more likely to generate taxonic findings than normal variation. The transfer is theirs, not mine.*

*One piece of advice was declined: to run a pilot measurement on a single person. An author measuring himself along axes he chose himself, in a test of his own hypothesis, is precisely the design that part four teaches the reader yields types in sixty cases out of ninety-two. "There is no measurement of my own" is an honest line; "there is a measurement of my own, and here it is" would be a dishonest one. Instead the death condition was sharpened so that an outsider can run the check.*
